# LiDAR model landscape briefing, October 20, 2026

- **Status:** Complete. No definition or default changed. One recommendation for the plans: capture the Pi 4 performance cells before budgeting any learned stage.
- **Scope:** Transformer, foundation and "physical AI" LiDAR models for fixed and moving sensors as of October 20, 2026; the harnesses and runtimes that run them; the Hugging Face inventory and the hardware its cards document; CPU-only and ARM feasibility at 10 and 20 Hz; public leaderboards and comparison sites.
- **Related:** [classifier plan](../../../plans/lidar-ml-classifier-training-plan.md), [cluster observation plan, Section 10](../../../plans/lidar-cluster-observation-log-and-async-tracking-plan.md), [clustering benchmark plan](../../../plans/lidar-clustering-observability-and-benchmark-plan.md), [performance regression testing](../performance-regression-testing.md), [AV LiDAR integration plan](../../../plans/lidar-av-lidar-integration-plan.md)

This briefing answers a survey question rather than a weekly watch: which learned LiDAR models
exist for a fixed sensor and for a moving one, what runs them, what Hugging Face holds, whether
any run CPU-only at 10 or 20 Hz, and where models are compared. Each number carries its source
and is marked measured or estimate; the sources were read on October 20, 2026, and the notes
behind them stay outside the repository. The short answer is that learned LiDAR perception still
needs a GPU.

As of 20 October 2026, no published transformer, foundation or "physical AI" LiDAR model runs on a Raspberry Pi 4 CPU at any usable rate, and nothing in the literature has been timed on a Pi 4 or any Cortex-A72 at all; the nearest measured ARM-CPU figures are Qualcomm's own benchmarks of a PointPillars-encoder CenterPoint at **0.86 to 6.4 seconds per frame** on Snapdragon cores that are three to ten times faster than the Pi's, which puts an unmodified pillar detector on the Pi 4 at tens of seconds per frame (an estimate, not a measurement). The state of the art for LiDAR-only detection is held by 2023 to 2024 architectures (LION, SAFDNet/HEDNet, DSVT at roughly 73 to 74 mAPH L2 on Waymo) whose only reported speeds are on RTX 3090-class or Jetson Orin GPUs, and every roadside benchmark in 2024 to 2026 still reports a PointPillars backbone. Documented 10 Hz learned detection begins at a Jetson Orin Nano class GPU (PointPillars at 12 FPS, measured) and documented 20 Hz begins at Jetson AGX Orin or Thor with TensorRT FP16 (PointPillars at 6.8 to 21 ms, measured); the only CPU route with a published 10 Hz number is Intel's 2021 OpenVINO proof-of-concept on an x86 Tiger Lake laptop chip, which does not transfer to ARM. Hugging Face holds about 46 LiDAR model repositories, of which the CPU-portable artefacts are Autoware's roughly 20 MB CenterPoint ONNX pairs, a 19 MB PointPillars safetensors, and two roadside OpenPCDet checkpoints; only one card (AMD's, x86) documents CPU inference and none mentions ARM64 Linux or a Pi. Public leaderboards exist for ego-vehicle datasets (nuScenes, Waymo, KITTI, Argoverse 2) but none is roadside, none ranks latency on fixed hardware, and the one aggregator that spanned them, Papers with Code, shut down in July 2025. The practical conclusion for velocity.report is that the Pi 4 is a classical-geometry host, that the field's own roadside work (beam-wise background subtraction at 6 to 39 ms on a desktop CPU) is the same architecture as the repository's L3 background model, that the only learned addition with a measured cost on Pi-class hardware is a per-cluster classifier, and that the upgrade which unlocks a learned detector is a Jetson Orin, not a faster CPU.

## Transformer detectors top out on Jetson Orin, and none has a published CPU path

The LiDAR-only detection frontier in late 2026 is still occupied by three 2023 to 2024 designs. LION, a window-based backbone built on linear group RNNs rather than attention, reports **75.9 mAP / 74.0 mAPH L2 on Waymo validation** for its largest Mamba variant and 73.9 NDS on the nuScenes test set, with a backbone of only 1.4 to 3.0 million parameters, but publishes no inference speed and admits its running speed "still needs further improvement" ([LION GitHub](https://github.com/happinesslz/LION); [LION arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.18232)). SAFDNet and HEDNet, fully sparse hierarchical encoder-decoders, reach 73.2 to 73.7 mAPH L2 on Waymo and 72.0 to 72.3 NDS on nuScenes test with no latency or GPU stated ([HEDNet/SAFDNet GitHub](https://github.com/zhanggang001/HEDNet)). DSVT, the dynamic sparse voxel transformer, is the one leaderboard-class model with usable timing: 67 ms for the pillar variant and 97 ms for the voxel variant in PyTorch, **37 ms with TensorRT on an RTX 3090** (about 27 Hz), trained on eight A100 40 GB cards ([DSVT GitHub](https://github.com/Haiyang-W/DSVT)). The only 2025 to 2026 detector paper with embedded-GPU latency is Qualcomm's FALO, which replaces sparse convolution and self-attention with large-kernel 1D convolutions over a serialised pillar sequence and reports **16.6 to 21.2 ms on a Jetson Orin GPU** and 33.8 to 41.7 ms on a Hexagon NPU at about one mAPH below DSVT; it has released neither code nor weights, so it is a reference point rather than an artefact ([FALO arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.04499v2)). The pattern across these four is that attention is no longer a prerequisite for leaderboard accuracy, and the two designs that care about latency (FlatFormer, FALO) minimise or drop it; "transformer LiDAR model" in 2026 is better read as "serialised sparse backbone".

Weight availability clusters on nuScenes and Argoverse 2, because DSVT, HEDNet/SAFDNet, VoxelNeXt, FlatFormer and PTv3 all state they cannot publish Waymo-trained weights under the Waymo Dataset License Agreement ([DSVT GitHub](https://github.com/Haiyang-W/DSVT); [VoxelNeXt GitHub](https://github.com/dvlab-research/VoxelNeXt); [PTv3 GitHub](https://github.com/Pointcept/PointTransformerV3)). Any fine-tuning path for a roadside sensor therefore starts from a nuScenes checkpoint, which means a 32-beam Velodyne on a moving car. For the roadside case specifically, every public infrastructure benchmark found reports a PointPillars family backbone: DAIR-V2X-I lists PointPillars at 63.07 / 54.00 / 54.01 vehicle AP (easy/moderate/hard) on a 300-beam infrastructure LiDAR ([DAIR-V2X paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2204.05575)); the A9/TUMTraf Intersection baseline is PointPillars at 40.89 mAP3D for a single Ouster OS1-64 on a 7 m gantry, rising to 51.25 with two fused LiDARs ([A9 Intersection arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.09266v1)); CoopDet3D's infrastructure-only LiDAR model reaches **84.88 mAP3D on the TUMTraf-V2X test set** with configs named `pointpillars512` and no FPS column ([CoopDet3D GitHub](https://github.com/tum-traffic-dataset/coopdet3d)); V2X-Real and RCooper likewise use PointPillars and report single-sensor AP in the 22 to 58 range depending on IoU threshold ([V2X-Real arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.16034); [RCooper arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.10145v2)). No LiDAR-only transformer trained and evaluated on a roadside dataset was found for 2025 or 2026. The 2026 roadside literature is instead about sensor configuration (MR-LiDAR's 16 to 128 beam comparison, which finds that "nominal beam count alone is insufficient" and that mounting height can change rankings), vehicle-to-roadside view synthesis (VRS), multi-resolution datasets (RESOLVE) and, most relevant here, classical per-beam background subtraction on a static sensor ([MR-LiDAR arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.24777); [VRS arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.05897); [RESOLVE arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.31895)).

That last line of work is the only roadside perception research with CPU timings. The HighwayScene study (ITSC 2026) benchmarks beam-wise Gaussian-mixture background subtraction on a static roadside Ouster OS0 at 10 Hz and reports **F1 of 99.43 with 5.95 to 38.74 ms per frame of spatial filtering on an AMD 9950X desktop CPU**, which it describes as compatible with real-time operation ([Beam-wise BGS arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.14868)). It is conceptually the same object as velocity.report's L3 EMA-plus-variance grid, and it produces foreground points rather than boxes, so downstream clustering and tracking remain classical. No learned detector in the catalogue uses the static background as a prior; the fixed-sensor advantage is exploited only by this classical line.

Foundation and self-supervised pretraining models exist but none was pretrained on roadside data. The closest sensor match to a Pandar40P is ScaLR (Valeo, CVPR 2024, Apache-2.0), which distils DINOv2 features into a WaffleIron backbone pretrained on nuScenes, SemanticKITTI and PandaSet (Hesai Pandar64 and PandarGT); its linear-probe mIoU drops from 67.8 on nuScenes to **37.9 on Pandar64**, a direct measure of how much a sensor and scene shift costs even within the same manufacturer family ([ScaLR GitHub](https://github.com/valeoai/ScaLR)). UniPAD (Apache-2.0) releases LiDAR pretrain and fine-tuned checkpoints and reports 70.6 NDS on nuScenes val ([UniPAD GitHub](https://github.com/Nightmare-n/UniPAD)). The better-known encoders are less usable: Point Transformer V3's released weights are "temporarily invalid as the model structure of PTv3 is adjusted" ([PTv3 GitHub](https://github.com/Pointcept/PointTransformerV3)), and Sonata's weights are CC-BY-NC 4.0, expect per-point colour and normals, and are demonstrated only on indoor scans ([Sonata GitHub](https://github.com/facebookresearch/sonata)). PTv3's own efficiency numbers (46.2M parameters, **44 ms inference and 1.2 GB on an RTX 4090** for nuScenes segmentation) set the scale of this family ([Point Transformer V3](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.10035)).

"Physical AI" in NVIDIA's usage is a generative and simulation story for LiDAR, not a perception one. The three NVIDIA LiDAR artefacts on Hugging Face are Cosmos-Transfer-LidarGen (a 28.9 GB diffusion model that takes three camera views and produces a LiDAR range map in "around 160 seconds" on an A100-80GB), the Cosmos LiDAR tokenizer, and LidarVerse, an October 2026 unified LiDAR world model whose distilled causal student runs at **11 FPS on a single H100** and whose released checkpoints are range-only models trained mostly on NVIDIA's internal 128-beam data ([nvidia/Cosmos-Transfer-LidarGen](https://huggingface.co/nvidia/Cosmos-Transfer-LidarGen); [nvidia/lidarverse](https://huggingface.co/nvidia/lidarverse); [LidarVerse arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.19201)). Cosmos 3, including the 4B "Edge" tier for Jetson AGX Orin and Thor, does not mention LiDAR as an input, output or tokenizer modality ([nvidia/cosmos GitHub](https://github.com/nvidia/cosmos)), and Alpamayo's inputs are cameras, text and ego-motion history with a 24 GB VRAM minimum ([nvidia/Alpamayo-R1-10B](https://huggingface.co/nvidia/Alpamayo-R1-10B)). In Cosmos-Drive-Dreams the measured benefit of synthetic LiDAR for detection training was about +1 mAP ([Cosmos-Drive-Dreams arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.09042v3)). None of this is a detector or a tracker, and none addresses a fixed infrastructure sensor.

On the tracking side, learned LiDAR trackers (3DMOTFormer's graph transformer at 71.2% AMOTA on nuScenes val with CenterPoint detections; LiDAR MOT-DETR at 0.722 to 0.724 aMOTA) are nuScenes research code with no published latency ([3DMOTFormer arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.06635v1); [LiDAR MOT-DETR arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.12753v2)). The roadside tracking benchmarks that exist run classical AB3DMOT on PointPillars and score **8 to 24% AMOTA**, which says the bottleneck on roadside data is detection quality on small datasets, not association ([RCooper arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.10145v2)). For speed measurement specifically, the quoted classical baseline is a UKF plus JPDA roadside tracker validated against an instrumented vehicle at 0.22 m/s mean speed accuracy ([IEEE Xplore 9200682](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/9200682)); no 2025 or 2026 paper applies transformers to roadside LiDAR speed estimation.

## Classical ICP still beats learned odometry, and a fixed sensor needs almost none of either

The motion and SLAM side of the landscape is relevant to a fixed roadside sensor in only two narrow ways, but the state of play is clear. No learned LiDAR odometry network beats classical point-to-point ICP on KITTI: the best transformer odometry, TransLO (2023), reports 0.993% translational error against **0.50% for KISS-ICP**, and every learned system found (LO-Net, PWCLO-Net, DeLORA) needs a discrete GPU ([TransLO](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/TransLO:-A-Window-Based-Masked-Point-Transformer-Liu-Wang/792798d7cfde416beedcae70cd5b5a92c4ab2737); [GenZ-ICP table](https://arxiv.org/html/2411.06766v1); [KISS-ICP arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.15397)). Neural-implicit SLAM trades accuracy or memory for GPU frame-rate operation: PIN-SLAM is quoted at 7 FPS on a 7 GB GPU by one survey and 29.4 FPS on an RTX 4090 by another, with a CPU mode that runs "much slower", while NeRF-LOAM needs about 4 seconds per frame ([survey 2402.13255](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.13255); [Hi-LOAM](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.01720); [PIN_SLAM GitHub](https://github.com/PRBonn/PIN_SLAM)). The CPU-only frontier is the KISS-ICP family (MIT, `pip install kiss-icp`, ROS 2, 38 Hz on KITTI-raw, CPU unspecified) and its loop-closing descendant KISS-SLAM, which a 2026 paper times at 13 ms per KITTI scan ([PRBonn/kiss-icp](https://github.com/PRBonn/kiss-icp); [alphaXiv overview](https://www.alphaxiv.org/overview/2209.15397); [HP2-SLAM](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.14996)). CT-ICP runs at about 60 ms per 64-beam scan on a single CPU thread and MAD-ICP's reported configuration uses 16 cores, so neither would hit 10 Hz on four Cortex-A72 cores without downsampling ([ct_icp GitHub](https://github.com/jedeschaud/ct_icp); [MAD-ICP arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.05828v1)).

The only quantitative ARM evidence is the 2022 TIERS benchmark, which ran FAST-LIO on a Jetson Xavier NX at 15 W at **14 to 23 Hz throughput using 54 to 73% of one core** with 330 to 370 MB of RAM ([TIERS benchmark](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.00812)). FAST-LIO2 lists "Raspberry Pi 4B (8G RAM)" among supported platforms, but that is a compatibility statement with no timing, it is GPL-2.0, and it requires a synchronised IMU that a fixed sensor does not carry ([hku-mars/FAST_LIO](https://github.com/hku-mars/FAST_LIO)). No source times KISS-ICP, KISS-SLAM, GenZ-ICP, MAD-ICP or CT-ICP on any ARM board. Transformer place recognition (OverlapTransformer, 0.906 Recall@1 on KITTI 00 at 3.28 ms descriptor extraction on an i7-11700K plus RTX 3070, GPL-3.0) and transformer registration (GeoTransformer, MIT, 6.2 cm RTE on KITTI, independently timed at **0.19 s per 5,000-point pair on a laptop RTX 3080 Ti**) are GPU tools with no CPU figures ([OverlapTransformer arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2203.03397v1); [GeoTransformer GitHub](https://github.com/qinzheng93/GeoTransformer); [CAST timing table](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.10295v1)). Scene flow and moving-object segmentation networks (DeFlow at 20.5 FPS, MotionBEV at 23 ms on an RTX 3090, 4DMOS at 132 ms) all depend on CUDA sparse-convolution libraries ([DeFlow](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.16122); [MotionBEV](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.07336); [KTH-RPL/OpenSceneFlow](https://github.com/KTH-RPL/OpenSceneFlow)).

For a static sensor the literature replaces the SLAM "map" with the per-beam statistical background already described, and the 2026 ISPRS roadside review describes the standard pipeline as background subtraction followed by clustering such as DBSCAN ([ScienceDirect roadside review](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0924271626000122)). Scene flow's job (which points moved and how fast) is what background subtraction plus Kalman tracking already yields per object. The two SLAM-adjacent pieces that still apply are one-off registration between two roadside sensors (where the review reports the field still using ICP and GMM methods) and detecting and correcting a sensor bump. For the bump case a cheap detector is the fraction of background cells suddenly classed foreground, and the correction is a single full-scan point-to-point ICP against the stored background using the KISS-ICP core; that is a one-off rather than a per-frame cost, so a sub-second CPU run would be acceptable, but no paper evaluates this procedure on a displaced roadside LiDAR, which is a gap rather than a finding.

## Every real-time harness is CUDA or TensorRT, and the CPU paths are pillar-only and slow

The research frameworks that LiDAR models ship in are mostly dormant and all CUDA-first. Pointcept (MIT) released v1.7.0 in March 2026 and is the only one actively developed; OpenPCDet's newest release note is still v0.6.0 from September 2022 with a single commit in October 2025; MMDetection3D has had no commit since v1.4.0 in January 2024; Det3D stopped in 2023 ([Pointcept README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Pointcept/Pointcept/main/README.md); [OpenPCDet commit feed](https://github.com/open-mmlab/OpenPCDet/commits/master.atom); [MMDetection3D commit feed](https://github.com/open-mmlab/mmdetection3d/commits/main.atom)). The sparse-convolution layer underneath voxel and transformer models is the real constraint: spconv's CPU wheel is Linux-only and its author says "you should only use this for debug usage", its performance not optimised and without OpenMP ([spconv README](https://github.com/traveller59/spconv)); TorchSparse documents GPU builds only ([TorchSparse README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mit-han-lab/torchsparse/master/README.md)); MinkowskiEngine is the only library advertising a supported CPU-only build and it has been unmaintained since March 2023 ([MinkowskiEngine README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/NVIDIA/MinkowskiEngine/master/README.md)). Pillar-based models (PointPillars, CenterPoint-pillar) avoid sparse convolution entirely, which is why they are the only family with any CPU route.

Three details in this table matter for a Go pipeline. First, the Intel figure, the only published CPU number at 10 Hz, comes from a repository that calls itself "not for production quality. It is just a proof of concept", that targets OpenVINO 2021.3, that lists an OpenCL compute runtime among its requirements (so whether the integrated GPU carried the backbone is unconfirmed), and that was archived in May 2025; the same article states that "the throughput requirement for the use cases of transportation infrastructure (e.g., 3D point clouds generated by the roadside Lidars) is 10 FPS", which is the one place in the sources where the roadside rate target is written down ([Intel OpenVINO PointPillars repository](https://github.com/intel/OpenVINO-optimization-for-PointPillars); [Intel article](https://www.intel.com/content/www/us/en/developer/articles/technical/optimization-of-pointpillars.html)). Second, pillar-family networks export to ONNX cleanly because the graph is dense 2D convolution plus scatter, and ONNX Runtime's CPU execution provider has kernels for ScatterND, ScatterElements, GatherND and NonMaxSuppression ([onnxruntime OperatorKernels](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/microsoft/onnxruntime/main/docs/OperatorKernels.md)); what does not export is the pillarisation front end and the box decoder, which every harness (NVIDIA, Intel, MMDeploy, Autoware) keeps outside the graph, and NVIDIA's TAO export contains "3 plugin layers that aren't directly supported by onnxruntime" that "all seem to pull in nvinfer and Cuda" ([NVIDIA forum](https://forums.developer.nvidia.com/t/using-pointpillar-onnx-model/283208); [NVIDIA forum, CPU request](https://forums.developer.nvidia.com/t/tao-pointpillars-for-cpu-infrence/299358)). Autoware's PTv3 export needs spconv TensorRT plugins, so there is no CPU ONNX path for anything in the sparse-conv or transformer family ([AutowareFoundation/ptv3](https://huggingface.co/AutowareFoundation/ptv3)). Third, the front end is the CPU bottleneck rather than the network: Autoware's `pointsToVoxels` took about 0.2 s per frame against 0.06 s of CenterPoint-tiny inference on a Jetson Orin, and a community PointPillars node cut a naive hard-voxel layer from about 239 ms to 1.76 ms with a bit-exact rewrite ([Autoware discussion #3386](https://github.com/orgs/autowarefoundation/discussions/3386); [LaserPerception on ROS Discourse](https://discourse.openrobotics.org/t/laserperception-v0-1-0-pointpillars-tensorrt-ros-2-with-honest-deployment-benchmarks/57386)). That is the piece a Go pipeline can own natively.

For Go itself the lowest-risk route is `yalue/onnxruntime_go` (v1.36.0, September 2026), which tracks the ONNX Runtime 1.29 C API and ships prebuilt test libraries for ARM64 Linux ([onnxruntime_go README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/yalue/onnxruntime_go/master/README.md); [proxy.golang.org](https://proxy.golang.org/github.com/yalue/onnxruntime_go/@latest)); the pure-Rust `tract` (0.23.9, hand-written NEON kernels) avoids a C shared library entirely but would need a sidecar ([tract README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/sonos/tract/main/README.md)); `gorgonia` is stale since October 2023. No public example of any of these running a point-cloud detector was found. The commercial roadside stacks (Ouster Gemini/BlueCity, trained on 4 million labelled objects from 800 sites and running on Jetson AGX Orin and Orin NX at more than 1,200 contracted sites by March 2026; Seoul Robotics SENSR) are closed, and the open cooperative-perception code (OpenCOOD, HEAL) carries a UCLA academic-only licence despite an MIT badge in the README ([Ouster blog](https://ouster.com/ja/insights/blog/physical-ai-ouster-geminis-ai-brings-powerful-capabilities-to-smart-infrastructure-industries); [BusinessWire Mar 2026](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260305627857/en/Ouster-Accelerates-Software-Adoption-in-2025-with-Record-Bookings-and-G); [OpenCOOD LICENSE](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DerrickXuNu/OpenCOOD/main/LICENSE)).

## Hugging Face holds about 46 LiDAR model repos, and only a handful are CPU-portable

A search of the Hub for "lidar" returns 46 model repositories, the most downloaded being `nvidia/Cosmos-Transfer-LidarGen` at 3,448 downloads in 30 days and `AutowareFoundation/lidar_centerpoint` at 866; by comparison the point-cloud LLM `RunsenXu/PointLLM_7B_v1.2` has 274 ([HF models search "lidar"](https://huggingface.co/models?search=lidar)). The supply comes from a few publishers: the Autoware Foundation (20 repos of ONNX detectors and segmenters for its ROS 2 nodes), the community library `torch-pointcloud` (149 safetensors re-hosts of OpenPCDet, Pointcept and MIT HAN Lab weights), Pointcept and Meta (foundation encoders), NVIDIA (three LiDAR repos, all generative) and silicon-vendor zoos (Qualcomm, AMD, Axera, Horizon, Renesas, Tenstorrent packaging) ([torch-pointcloud org](https://huggingface.co/torch-pointcloud); [huggingface.co/AutowareFoundation](https://huggingface.co/AutowareFoundation)). Hesai, Ouster, Waymo, Motional and TUM have no organisation presence, and the only "Hesai" hits are patent-text embeddings. The Hub is a mirror and packaging venue for LiDAR, not a release venue: OpenPCDet's model zoo still links Google Drive, no MMDetection3D mirror exists, PV-RCNN, DSVT, 2DPASS, CenterFormer, SAFDNet and HEDNet return zero repos by name, and no odometry, SLAM or 3D-tracking weights are hosted at all ([open-mmlab/OpenPCDet README](https://github.com/open-mmlab/OpenPCDet/blob/master/README.md); [HF models search "dsvt"](https://huggingface.co/models?search=dsvt)).

Two conclusions follow from the table. The CPU-portable artefacts for a Go pipeline are the Autoware CenterPoint ONNX pairs (small, Apache-2.0, documented tensor names, pre- and post-processing to be re-implemented because they live in the C++ node), torch-pointcloud's PointPillars safetensors (which would need conversion to ONNX), and the two roadside OpenPCDet checkpoints; everything else needs a CUDA sparse-conv extension or a vendor NPU toolchain. And the Hub documents hardware only for GPUs and NPUs: only AMD's card states validated CPU inference (x86), only Qualcomm's gives ARM-CPU timings, and **no card mentions Raspberry Pi, aarch64 Linux, ONNX Runtime on ARM64 or Go**. No repository trained on Pandar40P data exists; the only Pandar hit is a Pandar128 lane-line dataset. A related caveat is that Qualcomm's, AMD's and Renesas's repositories hold no weight files on the Hub itself (the binaries come from S3 or vendor scripts), so `hf download` alone does not retrieve them.

On the dataset side, only two genuinely roadside LiDAR sets with real data are on the Hub. HighwayScene (26 GB, CC BY-NC-SA 4.0, "Commercial use is not permitted") is a static roadside installation with an Ouster OS0 at 10 Hz, an Aeva FMCW unit and a Blickfeld solid-state unit over 5,998 frames of highway traffic, and ships rule-based static/dynamic labels rather than 3D boxes ([iis-esslingen/HighwayScene](https://huggingface.co/datasets/iis-esslingen/HighwayScene)); CoopScenes (524 GB, MIT) has tower-mounted Ouster OS2 LiDAR with automatically generated annotations ([iis-esslingen/CoopScenes](https://huggingface.co/datasets/iis-esslingen/CoopScenes)). TUMTraf, DAIR-V2X (full), V2X-Real raw data and RCooper are not on the Hub. The closest Hesai data is the PandaSet mirror (44.5 GB, CC-BY-4.0, Pandar64 and PandarGT, 16,000 sweeps) ([georghess/pandaset](https://huggingface.co/datasets/georghess/pandaset)). NVIDIA's gated 133 TB PhysicalAI-Autonomous-Vehicles set carries a licence worth reading before touching: it allows commercial AV development with NVIDIA technology but bans law-enforcement and road-traffic-enforcement use and expires 12 months after download ([nvidia/PhysicalAI-Autonomous-Vehicles](https://huggingface.co/datasets/nvidia/PhysicalAI-Autonomous-Vehicles)). The large ego-vehicle mirrors of nuScenes, SemanticKITTI and Waymo carry no licence metadata although the source licences are non-commercial or forbid redistribution. Hub tooling adds no help: there is no pipeline tag for 3D detection or point-cloud segmentation among the 52 tags, no `transformers` architecture for point clouds, every LiDAR demo Space found is in a RUNTIME_ERROR, BUILD_ERROR, PAUSED or SLEEPING state, and the only running LiDAR-related Spaces are Motional's nuScenes challenge evaluation servers ([HF models-tags-by-type](https://huggingface.co/api/models-tags-by-type); [nuscenes Spaces](https://huggingface.co/spaces?search=nuscenes)).

## The Pi 4 stays classical; 10 Hz learned detection starts at Orin Nano, 20 Hz at AGX Orin

The feasibility question has a clean answer once the measured numbers are separated from the extrapolations. The target frame is a Pandar40P at 720,000 single-return points per second, about 72,000 points per frame at 10 Hz and about 36,000 at 20 Hz, with repository captures recorded at 69,532 points per frame ([cluster observation plan](../../../plans/lidar-cluster-observation-log-and-async-tracking-plan.md); [scene catalogue plan](../../../plans/lidar-scene-catalogue-publishing-plan.md)). That sits between nuScenes (about 22,000 points, 32 channels) and SemanticKITTI (about 90,000, 64 channels), so published KITTI numbers are pessimistic for this frame and nuScenes numbers optimistic ([embedded segmentation benchmark](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.08365)).

The Pi 4 row is the one that matters most and it is an estimate because no source has run any scene-level LiDAR model on a Pi 4 or 5, with or without the AI HAT+. The estimate rests on the Snapdragon CPU table: 5.1 seconds per frame on a 2022 flagship phone core and 0.86 seconds on a 2025 one for a 21.8M-parameter pillar model at 1.8 GB peak memory, on cores that are several times faster than a 1.5 GHz Cortex-A72 ([qualcomm/CenterPoint](https://huggingface.co/qualcomm/CenterPoint)). Memory is not the binding constraint on a 4 GB Pi (a 1.8 GB inference process plus the Go pipeline fits); CPU time is, by roughly two orders of magnitude. Even a Pi 5's Cortex-A76 cores are unlikely to bring a full-scene PointPillars under one second per frame without heavy pruning (estimate). INT8 does not rescue this: every INT8 LiDAR result found is TensorRT or NPU, and on Orin INT8 post-training quantisation collapses PointPillars accuracy (13.12 mAP) unless quantisation-aware training is used (58.24), for a saving of about 20% over FP16 ([arXiv 2601.12638](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.12638v2)).

The repository's own Pi evidence should be read with the same care. The clustering plan sets a target budget for the classical stack on a Pi 4 at 10 Hz of 55 ms (polar conversion 2, background update 15, ground filter 2, DBSCAN 25, tracking 8, serialisation 3) against a configured 98 ms frame budget, but that table is a target to be calibrated by running the benchmark on a Pi, not a measurement ([clustering benchmark plan](../../../plans/lidar-clustering-observability-and-benchmark-plan.md), section C.2). The committed performance baselines were captured on an M1 Mac (kirk0 `full`: mean 8.1 ms, p95 34.3 ms, p99 77.9 ms), the performance reference says the Pi is "roughly an order of magnitude" slower, and the cluster-observation plan's own extrapolation by that factor puts the Pi's L3 plus L4 p95 at **about 340 ms before any tracker work**, concluding that "either the factor is pessimistic for this workload or the device already drops frames on busy scenes" and that this is "the first measurement to take" ([cluster observation plan](../../../plans/lidar-cluster-observation-log-and-async-tracking-plan.md), sections 10.1 and 10.4; [performance regression testing](../performance-regression-testing.md)). So the headroom available on the Pi for any learned addition is unknown until the Pi cells of the performance matrix are captured, and the classical stack's own 10 Hz compliance on busy frames is itself unverified.

Within that uncertainty, the learned additions with a measured cost on Pi-class ARM CPUs are per-cluster classifiers on the output of the existing DBSCAN: PointNet at about 237 ms per cluster on a Pi 4 and about 21 ms on a Pi 5 ([arXiv 2608.07106](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.07106)), and on a Coral Dev Board (quad Cortex-A53 plus Edge TPU, 1 GB RAM) a 2D-projection CNN on each cluster at 1.07 ms and an autoencoder at 1.25 ms after INT8 quantisation, with 95.8% classification accuracy for the CNN ([arXiv 2311.18077](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2311.18077)). At 20 to 50 clusters per frame, the Pi 4 PointNet figure does not fit a 98 ms frame synchronously; a 2D-projection CNN or an asynchronous classifier on a second core could. A reduced-width range-image CNN at the Pandar40P's native 40 rings is the other plausible CPU target by analogy with SalsaNext (roughly 1.6 to 3.2 times smaller than the 64x2048 model, which on a QCS6490-class CPU would scale the 2.0 s figure to roughly 0.6 to 1.2 s), still well short of 10 Hz on any ARM CPU without an NPU (estimate). Pre-processing deserves its own line: on GPUs voxelisation is 0.18 to 1.4 ms, but on CPUs a naive pillariser is 200 to 240 ms and range-image projection for SalsaNext costs 58 ms on Orin's CPU for a 90k-point frame, 53 to 69% of the end-to-end time ([CUDA-PointPillars](https://github.com/NVIDIA-AI-IOT/CUDA-PointPillars); [Autoware discussion #3386](https://github.com/orgs/autowarefoundation/discussions/3386); [embedded segmentation benchmark](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.08365)). A single-pass O(N) pillariser in Go, in the style of the repository's existing polar conversion and background grid, should be a few milliseconds per frame on a Pi 4 (estimate from the plan's 2 ms polar and 15 ms grid budgets), which is why the front end is the part worth owning natively if any ONNX detector is ever run on a bigger host.

The upgrade tiers divide cleanly on measured evidence. A Pi 5 with the AI HAT+ 2 (Hailo-10H, 40 TOPS, 8 GB on-board, US$130) has no point-cloud result anywhere; the Hailo Model Zoo contains no LiDAR model, and the one Hailo-8 PointPillars deployment reached about 5 Hz with pillarisation on an unstated host ([Hailo PUBLIC_MODELS](https://github.com/hailo-ai/hailo_model_zoo/blob/master/docs/PUBLIC_MODELS.rst); [arXiv 2412.05594](https://arxiv.org/html/2412.05594v1); [Tecnoblog](https://tecnoblog.net/noticias/raspberry-pi-anuncia-placa-ai-hat-2-com-40-tops-para-ia-preco-e-alto/)). A Jetson Orin Nano 8 GB has a measured 12 FPS for PointPillars (framework unstated) and runs out of memory on voxel CenterPoint, so PointPillars at 10 Hz is likely and 20 Hz plausible for a 36k-point frame with TensorRT FP16, but that is an inference from the Xavier-class 31 to 39 FPS TAO numbers, not a direct measurement ([arXiv 2508.00744](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.00744); [TAO PointPillarNet](https://catalog.ngc.nvidia.com/orgs/nvidia/teams/tao/models/pointpillarnet)). Orin NX and AGX Orin are where PointPillars at 20 Hz and CenterPoint at 10 Hz are measured, at 15 to 40 W of compute, and this is also where Autoware's nodes, NVIDIA's aarch64 libspconv builds and Ouster's own BlueCity appliances run, so it is the "same ARM64 architecture, add a GPU" path. Prices moved against it in 2026: NVIDIA raised Jetson prices by up to 101% in July (Orin NX 8 GB $399 to $649, 16 GB $599 to $999, AGX Orin dev kit $1,999 to $3,499), while the Pi 5 8 GB rose to $175 and 16 GB to $305 by April; the Orin Nano Super dev kit was $249 at its December 2024 launch and its post-July price could not be read ([CNX Software](https://www.cnx-software.com/2026/07/22/nvidia-increases-the-price-of-jetson-modules-and-devkits-by-up-to-101/); [BGR](https://www.bgr.com/2150106/raspberry-pi-cost-ram-crisis/); [JetsonHacks](https://jetsonhacks.com/2024/12/17/jetson-orin-nano-super-developer-kit/)). On power, measured detector runs draw about 6 W on a Jetson Nano, 11 to 14 W on Xavier NX and 21 to 29 W on AGX Xavier, an Orin NX roadside node draws at most 30 W of compute, and a Pi 4 draws roughly 1 to 3 W idle and 6 W loaded, so an Orin Nano at 15 W plus the LiDAR fits an 802.3at PoE budget and the larger Orin tiers need 802.3bt (the PoE arithmetic is an estimate; LiDAR power was not sourced) ([Sensors 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10144830/); [arXiv 2509.20009](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.20009); [Zbotic](https://zbotic.in/raspberry-pi-5-benchmarks-real-performance-numbers-vs-pi-4/)).

The classical comparison closes the argument. A CPU-only roadside tracker (ground threshold, crop box, voxel filter, Euclidean clustering, L-shape fit, Hungarian association, constant-acceleration Kalman) runs at **25.5 ms per 10 Hz frame on an Orin NX's eight Cortex-A78AE cores at about 40% peak CPU**, and the beam-wise background subtraction runs at 6 to 39 ms on a desktop CPU; both are one to two orders of magnitude cheaper than any learned detector on the same silicon ([arXiv 2509.20009](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.20009); [Beam-wise BGS](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.14868)). On 10 Hz versus 20 Hz specifically: 20 Hz learned detection is measured only on AGX Orin or Thor with TensorRT FP16 PointPillars; 10 Hz is measured on Xavier NX, AGX Xavier and Orin Nano with pillar models and on AGX Orin with voxel CenterPoint; a community PointPillars node sustained 10 Hz but not 15 or 20 Hz even on an RTX 4060 Laptop GPU with multi-sweep inputs ([LaserPerception on ROS Discourse](https://discourse.openrobotics.org/t/laserperception-v0-1-0-pointpillars-tensorrt-ros-2-with-honest-deployment-benchmarks/57386)); nothing CPU-only on ARM reaches 1 Hz for a full-scene detector. At 20 Hz the Pandar40P halves both the points per frame and the frame budget, so the per-frame compute ratio is unchanged and the only thing 20 Hz buys a classical pipeline is finer temporal sampling at the same CPU load per second.

## Leaderboards rank ego-vehicle GPU models; none ranks roadside sensors or latency

Four ego-vehicle detection and tracking leaderboards are confirmed or presumed to still accept submissions. Waymo states on a page fetched on 20 October 2026 that "the leaderboards remain fully active" with no formal 2026 challenge, but 3D LiDAR detection and tracking were last challenged in 2020, the ranking metric is mAPH L2 with no latency or hardware column, and the licence is non-commercial with a Derivative IP clause on trained models ([Waymo challenges](https://waymo.com/open/challenges/); [Waymo terms](https://waymo.com/open/terms/)). nuScenes' detection, tracking, lidarseg and panoptic evaluation servers run as Hugging Face Spaces on `cpu-basic` hardware, last modified August 2026, and the most recent LiDAR-only claim is SEGT's **74.5 NDS with test-time augmentation** on the test set, "1st place in the nuScenes lidar-based object detection task" as of January 2025 ([nuscenes Spaces](https://huggingface.co/spaces?search=nuscenes); [SEGT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.09658)). KITTI now accepts only submissions that "differ significantly from prior work and could be published as a peer-reviewed paper", deletes anonymous entries after six months, and is the only board with a runtime column, self-reported and hardware-heterogeneous ([KITTI policy page](https://cvlibs.net/datasets/kitti/user_login.php)). Argoverse 2's boards live on eval.ai, which was unreachable; its paper's leaderboard snapshot and aggregator rows give LION at 41.5 mAP as the LiDAR-only reference ([Argoverse 2 paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2301.00493)). ONCE, Lyft, ZOD and MAN TruckScenes have no confirmed live leaderboard. All of these are moving-sensor, 32- to 64-beam ego-vehicle datasets at 10 or 20 Hz; the nearest in sparsity to a Pandar40P are nuScenes (32 beams, 20 Hz) and Argoverse 2 (two 32-beam units, 10 Hz), and their value for this project is a sanity check that a model family is competitive on sparse data, not a ranking for the roadside task.

No roadside or cooperative dataset runs an open, continuously accepting leaderboard. DAIR-V2X had a one-off 2022 challenge on Baidu AI Studio and planned 2025 workshop challenges; its official benchmark page still reads "DAIR-V2X is currently at the invitation-only stage" and the GitHub repository states there is no test server ([DAIR-V2X repo](https://github.com/AIR-THU/DAIR-V2X); [DAIR-V2X benchmark page](https://air.tsinghua.edu.cn/DAIR-V2X/english/benchmark.html)). TUMTraf, V2X-Real, RCooper, OPV2V, IPS300+, LUMPI, Ko-PER, BAAI-VANJEE, HighwayScene, R-LiViT and RESOLVE publish results as README or paper tables; CoopDet3D's README is the de-facto TUMTraf comparison table ([CoopDet3D repo](https://github.com/tum-traffic-dataset/coopdet3d)). The only 2026 roadside challenge with a scored submission process is the UCF UrbanTwin Sim2Real LiDAR cup at IEEE BigData 2026, with LUMPI and V2X-Real tracks scored on 50 held-out real frames, where a LUMPI-track entry reports a combined 0.4692 ([UrbanTwin](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.06781); [LUMPI-track solution](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.07590)). On sensor geometry the most representative public benchmarks for a fixed Pandar40P are TUMTraf-I/A9 (two OS1-64 at 10 Hz on a 7 m gantry), LUMPI (VLP-16, HDL-64, Hesai Pandar64 and PandarQT at a Hanover junction, CC BY-NC 3.0) and HighwayScene (OS0 at 10 Hz, static background subtraction); DAIR-V2X-I is far denser at 300 beams, so its AP will not transfer ([A9 paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.09266); [Cooperative Infrastructure Perception](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2207.08930); [DAIR-V2X paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2204.05575)). Every roadside dataset found is licensed non-commercially (CC BY-NC-ND or BY-NC-SA) except R-LiViT (CC0), which constrains any weights trained on them that would ship in a product ([R-LiViT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.17122)).

The aggregator layer has collapsed. Meta shut Papers with Code down on 24 to 25 July 2025 without notice; the domain redirects to Hugging Face Trending Papers, the leaderboards did not carry over, and the rescued data sits in the `pwc-archive` Hugging Face organisation (evaluation tables of about 2,250 rows, last updated between August and December 2025) and in third-party mirrors whose tables stop at mid-2025 and mix test and validation splits ([HyperAI news](https://hyper.ai/en/news/42900); [pwc-archive](https://huggingface.co/datasets/pwc-archive)). HF Papers indexes 120 LiDAR papers but has no leaderboards, and LiDAR papers there attract 0 to 13 upvotes while generic world-model papers draw hundreds, a fair proxy for where future weight uploads will go ([HF Papers search "lidar"](https://huggingface.co/papers?q=lidar)). The maintained cross-model comparisons that remain are the OpenPCDet and MMDetection3D model zoos, which are reproducible (same codebase, same split) but carry no FPS column, and Autoware's AWML, which publishes no latency on its README ([OpenPCDet README](https://github.com/open-mmlab/OpenPCDet/blob/master/README.md); [MMDetection3D model zoo](https://github.com/open-mmlab/mmdetection3d/blob/main/docs/en/model_zoo.md); [AWML](https://github.com/tier4/AWML)). MLPerf Automotive, the one standardised latency benchmark in this space, has no LiDAR task: its 3D detection reference is camera-only BEVFormer-tiny on nuScenes with a 100 ms V100 reference latency measured at the 99.9th percentile ([MLPerf Automotive paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.27065v2); [mlperf_automotive repo](https://github.com/mlcommons/mlperf_automotive)). The only fixed-hardware LiDAR latency tables anywhere are NVIDIA's Orin READMEs and the one Orin Nano paper; nothing ranks ARM64 CPU inference, so any feasibility claim for the Pi tier has to come from first-party measurement.

## Conclusion

The landscape resolves into one structural fact: the static background is the fixed sensor's decisive advantage, and no learned LiDAR model exploits it. The whole transformer, foundation and "physical AI" catalogue was built for a moving ego vehicle where the scene must be re-perceived every frame; the roadside field has answered the same problem with per-beam temporal statistics that run in milliseconds on a CPU, and velocity.report's L3 to L6 stack is that answer written in Go. The right reading of the 2026 evidence is therefore not that the Pi 4 is too weak for modern perception but that modern perception, as published, solves a harder problem than a fixed roadside sensor has. What the learned literature does offer a fixed sensor is narrow and specific: a per-cluster classifier whose cost on Pi-class hardware is actually measured, a native O(N) pillariser as the one component worth owning if an ONNX detector is ever run on a bigger host, and ScaLR's Pandar64 pretraining as the nearest permissively licensed starting point should a GPU host and a labelled roadside set ever coincide.

The decisions this implies are concrete. The first measurement to take is the repository's own Pi 4 performance cells, because the 55 ms figure is a target and the plan's own extrapolation suggests the classical stack may already exceed its 98 ms budget on busy frames; until that is known, no learned addition can be budgeted. If a learned detector becomes a product requirement, the host change is a Jetson Orin (Nano for 10 Hz pillar detection on the evidence, NX or AGX for measured 20 Hz), accepted with the July 2026 price rise and a licence review of whatever nuScenes- or roadside-trained weights it would carry, since every roadside dataset and nuScenes itself are non-commercial. And the comparison surface this project needs does not exist publicly: there is no leaderboard for a 40-beam static sensor on an ARM64 CPU, so the MLPerf Automotive protocol (99.9th-percentile tail latency at a constant 10 or 20 Hz stream) is the template to adopt for reporting the repository's own frame budget, with the results of such runs recorded in `docs/` as the engineering record rather than sought in a ranking elsewhere.
